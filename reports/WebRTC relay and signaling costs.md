# One Cloudflare account covers Sanguis' servers for $0-5

For Sanguis' traffic, a "server in the middle" costs almost nothing. At 100, 1,000 or 10,000 matches a month, a TURN relay, a signaling server, or a WebSocket server that relays every game message can each run for **$0 on free tiers**. The most robust all-in-one setup is a flat **$5/month** on Cloudflare's Workers Paid plan, which bundles a Worker, Durable Objects and the credential minting for Cloudflare's TURN service. Data volume is never the constraint. By my estimate a relayed card match moves about 0.5-1 MB including WebRTC keepalives, and a signaling exchange moves only 2-10 KB. Even at 10,000 matches a month that comes to single-digit gigabytes, while TURN free tiers run from 500 MB up to 1,000 GB. The real costs are elsewhere. GitHub Pages cannot keep a secret, so any TURN service with short-lived credentials needs a small serverless endpoint. Some free tiers sleep or pause (Supabase after a week of inactivity, Render after 15 minutes, Oracle reclaims idle VMs). And every new server sees players' IP addresses, which brings GDPR duties and abuse risk. The cheapest viable path is about **$0/month**: Cloudflare TURN credentials minted by a free Worker, plus a free Durable Objects signaling room, with copy-paste kept as a fallback. The robust path is about **$5/month**: the same stack on Workers Paid, with an optional WebSocket relay fallback. One caveat applies to every figure here. The research sessions could not load vendor pricing pages directly. Most numbers come from search-result extracts and must be re-checked before money is committed. A table at the end says which is which.

## Where Sanguis stands today and what each server would fix

Today the game uses a fully serverless handshake. The host's invite link is the page plus `#invite=<SDP offer>`. The friend pastes a reply code back. The only ICE servers are Google's and Cloudflare's public STUN servers (`/home/user/test-webrtc/index.html`, lines 11 and 262). The owner pays nothing for this, but it has two gaps. First, connections fail whenever NAT traversal fails. Second, players have to pass two codes by hand. Three kinds of server close these gaps in different ways. A **TURN relay** fixes failed connections and keeps the peer-to-peer design. A **signaling server** replaces copy-paste with a room link, but it does not help when the networks themselves block direct connections. A **WebSocket game relay** fixes both at once, because every message goes through the server and the connection works anywhere a `wss://` connection on port 443 works.

How often does TURN matter? Nobody has published an authoritative figure. A small 2026 study cited by OpenVidu found **up to 17.7% of sessions relayed** ([OpenVidu](https://openvidu.io/blog/2026/06/09/turn-key-considerations/)). Vendor estimates run from **10-25%** ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/video-streaming/articles-streaming/turn-bandwidth-calculator); [ExpressTURN](https://www.expressturn.com/streaming)). Older EasyRTC docs, without a source, say 85-90% of connections need no TURN ([EasyRTC](https://github.com/gunjank/easyrtc/blob/master/docs/easyrtc_server_ice.md)). The Trystero README says flatly that "some networks simply don't allow direct P2P connections" ([Trystero](https://github.com/dmotz/trystero)). In short, without TURN roughly **one match in five or six risks failing to connect**. That makes TURN the most valuable addition, ahead of convenience features.

Bandwidth is tiny, but this figure is an estimate. I assumed a 15-minute match of about 200 JSON messages of about 200 bytes each. With per-packet DTLS/SCTP/UDP overhead that is about 80 KB of game traffic. On top of that, ICE consent checks sent every ~5 seconds ([RFC 7675](https://rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7675)) add about 85 KB, which makes keepalives larger than the game itself. The total is about 0.2 MB per relayed leg, and up to about 0.8 MB if both peers force relay mode. The cost models below plan on **1 MB per relayed match** as a safe upper bound. That estimate should be replaced with a measurement: read `getStats()` byte counters on the candidate pair during one real match.

## TURN relays: Cloudflare's 1,000 GB free tier makes the meter irrelevant

**Cloudflare Realtime TURN** charges **$0.05/GB after the first 1,000 GB each month** ([Cloudflare Realtime pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/pricing/)). The free allowance is shared with Cloudflare's SFU product. Only edge-to-client egress is billed, and that count includes TURN overhead. STUN is free ([Cloudflare TURN FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/faq/)). Cloudflare runs TURN over anycast, so the relay sits near each player, and it refuses to relay to private or loopback addresses ([Calls TURN FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/calls/turn/faq)). It also offers `turns:` on TCP 443 for networks that only allow HTTPS. Credentials come from a server-side POST to `rtc.live.cloudflare.com/v1/turn/keys/{key_id}/credentials/…` with a Bearer secret and a `ttl`. Cloudflare says to keep the key on the server ([generate credentials](https://developers.cloudflare.com/calls/turn/generate-credentials)). The sources disagree on whether the current path ends in `/generate` or `/generate-ice-servers`. The 48-hour maximum TTL comes only from a third-party copy of the docs.

The alternatives each have a catch. **Twilio** has no free TURN tier. It charges **$0.40/GB** in the US and Europe and **$0.60-0.80/GB** in APAC and South America. It mints 24-hour credentials from an account-wide Auth Token ([Twilio NTS pricing](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/stun-turn/pricing); [Twilio API](https://www.twilio.com/docs/stun-turn/api)). **Metered's** free trial covers **500 MB** (ingress plus egress). The next step is the **$99/month** Growth plan ([Metered pricing](https://metered.ca/pricing)). Metered stands out because it issues a credential-scoped apiKey that it calls "safe for front-end use", so a static page can fetch ICE servers with no backend at all ([Metered get credentials](https://metered.ca/docs/turn-rest-api/get-credentials-v2)). Metered's **Open Relay** advertises **20 GB/month free**, but with published shared credentials ([Open Relay](https://www.metered.ca/tools/openrelay/)). The free-tier figures also conflict: older Metered posts say 5 GB, 50 GB or 50 GB respectively. **Xirsys** (500 MB free, a $39 Basic plan) and **ExpressTURN** (claims 1 TB free and $9 for 5 TB) could only be confirmed from aggregators and the vendor's own marketing ([freetier.co](https://freetier.co/directory/products/xirsys); [ExpressTURN vs Metered](https://www.expressturn.com/vs-metered)).

**Self-hosting coturn** costs a flat fee, and the owner takes on operations work. DigitalOcean's $4 Droplet includes 500 GiB of transfer, with overage at $0.01/GiB ([DO bandwidth FAQ](https://docs.digitalocean.com/release-notes/upcoming/droplet-bandwidth-billing-faq); price from [infratally](https://infratally.com/articles/digitalocean-droplet-pricing-guide-2026/), a third party). Hetzner's CX23 costs roughly **$5-7/month** including IPv4, with 20 TB of EU traffic. Hetzner raised prices twice in 2026, and its cost-optimized plans showed "Currently not available" as of August ([findstack](https://findstack.com/resources/hetzner-price-increase-2026); [Hetzner traffic](https://docs.hetzner.com/robot/general/traffic/)). Oracle's Always Free tier costs $0 and includes 10 TB of egress. However, it reclaims instances that stay under 20% utilisation for 7 days, and an idle TURN box would sit under that line ([selfhost.it](https://selfhost.it.com/blog/oci-free-tier-breakdown)). Fly.io needs a dedicated IPv4 and a special `fly-global-services` bind for UDP ([Fly docs](https://fly.io/docs/networking/udp-and-tcp)). A self-hosted coturn also needs these pieces: a domain and a Let's Encrypt certificate for TURNS on 443, open ports 3478/5349/443, a relay port range, `use-auth-secret` HMAC credentials ([coturn man page](https://manpages.opensuse.org/Tumbleweed/coturn/coturn.1.en.html)), and an ongoing patch schedule. The patching is a real cost, as the security section shows.

Every option except Metered's apiKey and Open Relay's public credentials needs the same thing from the owner: **a tiny HTTPS endpoint, such as a Cloudflare Worker on a free `*.workers.dev` subdomain, that holds the secret and returns short-lived ICE servers** to the GitHub Pages site. GitHub Pages itself stays unchanged.

## Signaling servers: free tiers dwarf a 2-10 KB handshake

A signaling exchange is two connections, an offer and an answer, and with trickle ICE about 3-10 candidates per side. I estimate about **4 messages and 3 KB without trickle, and about 25 messages and 10 KB with it**. At those sizes the binding limit is concurrent connections. Message quotas never come close.

**Cloudflare Workers + Durable Objects** fit best. The free plan runs SQLite-backed Durable Objects with **100,000 requests/day and 13,000 GB-s/day**. Once a limit is hit, further operations fail; they are not billed ([DO pricing source](https://github.com/cloudflare/cloudflare-docs/blob/production/src/content/partials/durable-objects/durable-objects-pricing.mdx)). A WebSocket connection counts as 1 request. Incoming messages are billed 20:1, and outgoing messages are free. With the Hibernation API, idle sockets incur no duration charge. A match therefore costs about 4 requests, so 10,000 matches is about 1,300 requests a day. The paid tier costs a **$5/month minimum** that covers Workers, KV and Durable Objects, with 1M requests included ([Workers pricing, via search](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)). **PartyServer**, the library form of PartyKit, runs on the same Durable Objects at the same price ([cloudflare/partykit](https://github.com/cloudflare/partykit)). A search summary of the PartyKit docs says **hosted PartyKit and `npx partykit deploy` stop working on 23 October 2026**, which is tomorrow relative to this report ([migration guide, via search](https://docs.partykit.io/guides/migrate-to-partyserver/)). The live docs could not be fetched, and the GitHub README does not mention a shutdown. Either way, the owner should not build on hosted PartyKit. If PartyKit's API is wanted, the owner should deploy PartyServer with wrangler on their own Cloudflare account.

The no-server-code alternatives all work at $0 but come with constraints. On **Firebase Realtime Database Spark**, the free plan allows 100 simultaneous connections and 10 GB of downloads a month, and it shuts off rather than bills when a quota is exceeded ([Magora](https://magora-systems.com/firebase-pricing/), a third party). **Supabase Realtime** free includes about 200 concurrent connections and 2M messages, but **free projects pause after a week of inactivity** and must be restored by hand ([Supabase pricing, via search](https://supabase.com/pricing); [mykavo](https://mykavo.app/blog/supabase-free-tier-limits)). For a bursty hobby game this means signaling silently breaks after a quiet week. **Ably's** free tier includes 6M messages a month and 200 connections ([Ably free package, via search](https://ably.com/docs/platform/pricing/free)). **Pusher's** free tier includes 200k messages a day and 100 connections ([Pusher, via search](https://pusher.com/channels/)). **Trystero** needs no account at all. It discovers peers through public Nostr relays (hundreds of them), MQTT brokers or BitTorrent trackers, or through the owner's own Firebase or Supabase project ([Trystero](https://github.com/dmotz/trystero)). **PeerJS cloud** is free but has no SLA, and users have reported connection delays of 6+ minutes while its status page showed all systems operational ([peerjs #1350](https://github.com/peers/peerjs/issues/1350)). A self-hosted PeerServer runs about $2.19/month on Fly ([Fly pricing](https://docs.fly.io/about/pricing)) or about $5 on Railway Hobby ([Railway](https://docs.railway.com/pricing)). Render's free tier spins down after 15 minutes idle and takes about a minute to wake ([Render](https://render.com/docs/free)). Vercel's WebSocket support is still a public beta with unknown limits ([Vercel KB, via search](https://vercel.com/kb/guide/do-vercel-serverless-functions-support-websocket-connections)).

## A WebSocket game relay is cheap only if it hibernates

Relaying every game message through a Durable Object removes NAT problems entirely. It hides players' IPs from each other, and it lets the server become the authority (deal from a server-side RNG, keep each hand hidden, validate moves). By my estimate a match is about 12 billed requests: 2 connections plus 200 incoming messages at 20:1. At 10,000 matches that is about 120k requests a month, which is **$0 on the free plan or within the $5 paid minimum** ([DO pricing source](https://github.com/cloudflare/cloudflare-docs/blob/production/src/content/partials/durable-objects/durable-objects-pricing.mdx)). The trap is duration billing. Plain `accept()` WebSockets bill 128 MB of memory for the whole connection. A 20-minute match then uses 150 GB-s, which caps the free plan at about **86 matches/day**. At 10,000 matches/month it pushes the paid bill to about **$30**: 1.5M GB-s, minus 400k included, with the remainder rounded up to 2M at $12.50/M, plus the $5 minimum. The Hibernation API brings duration close to zero. A $4-7 VPS also handles thousands of concurrent small-message matches, but the owner then maintains TLS, a process manager and updates.

This changes the architecture, and the owner should weigh that. The server sees all game data, unless the clients add their own encryption layer, which none of the research covered. An outage stops every game. The game also gives up its "no game server" premise. A **hybrid** design is worth considering: use the Durable Object for signaling, and fall back to the same Durable Object as a message relay only when ICE fails. That replaces a TURN vendor with code the owner already runs.

## Security and privacy: the servers add new attack paths and new personal data

**TURN credentials in a static page are public.** The TURN REST API draft says it outright: keeping TURN credentials secret "is typically impossible" in web apps, and time-limited HMAC credentials are the standard fix ([draft-uberti-behave-turn-rest-00](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-uberti-behave-turn-rest-00)). TURN relays are attractive open proxies. Threat actors have abused them since at least 2017, and in 2026 Symantec reported ransomware tunnelling its command-and-control traffic through Microsoft Teams TURN relays ([Enable Security](https://www.enablesecurity.com/newsletter/2026-06-rtcsec-news/)). The minting Worker should therefore use short TTLs (10-60 minutes rather than the usual 24 hours), the Workers rate-limit binding ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/runtime-apis/bindings/rate-limit)), and optionally Turnstile. Ideally it should mint credentials only after a direct connection attempt fails. An Origin check only filters browsers, because a script can send any Origin header. Self-hosted coturn carries heavier risk. Slack's TURN servers once relayed traffic to the AWS metadata endpoint and leaked IAM credentials ([Enable Security](https://www.enablesecurity.com/blog/slack-webrtc-turn-compromise-and-bug-bounty/)). coturn has had repeated loopback-filter bypasses, most recently **CVE-2026-53450** (fixed in 4.13.0) ([cvefeed](https://cvefeed.io/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-53450)). It can also act as a UDP amplifier unless the new `unauthorized-ratelimit` option in 4.14.0 is enabled ([Enable Security](https://www.enablesecurity.com/newsletter/2026-06-rtcsec-news/)). A self-hosted coturn needs the latest release, explicit `denied-peer-ip` ranges including 169.254.0.0/16 and IPv4-mapped forms, and per-user quotas.

**The signaling server sits in the trust path.** DTLS is end-to-end only if the SDP fingerprints arrive unmodified ([RFC 8826](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8826.html); [LiveSwitch](https://developer.liveswitch.io/liveswitch-server/server/security.html)). A compromised server, or anyone who guesses a room code and joins first, can therefore hijack or man-in-the-middle a match. The countermeasures are 128-bit random room IDs in the link fragment, rooms that close after two participants, a host-side "accept this player?" prompt, and optionally a short code derived from both `a=fingerprint` lines that players compare (my inference from the RFC threat model). WebSocket servers must check `Origin` to block cross-site WebSocket hijacking ([OWASP WSTG](https://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide/latest/4-Web_Application_Security_Testing/11-Client-side_Testing/10-Testing_WebSockets)). On Firebase and Supabase the client key is not a secret, so security rules are the only boundary. Firebase's test-mode rules let "anyone … read and overwrite" data ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.cn/docs/database/web/start?hl=fi)). Supabase public channels accept any subscriber unless private channels backed by RLS are enabled ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/authorization)). Firebase budget alerts do not pause Realtime Database or Firestore ([Firebase budget alerts](https://firebase.google.com/docs/projects/billing/budget-alerts)). Trystero's default SDP encryption key can be reverse-engineered by relay operators unless the app sets a `password` ([Trystero](https://github.com/dmotz/trystero)). If the owner adopts Trystero, the password should be a random secret placed in the invite fragment.

**Privacy shifts more than it worsens.** The current invite link already carries the host's public (srflx) IP, and Google and Cloudflare STUN already see both players. A TURN operator sees IPs, timing and byte counts but cannot read the DTLS-encrypted data channel ([Metered](https://www.metered.ca/docs/turn-server-service/what-is-turn/)). A WebSocket relay sees everything. `iceTransportPolicy: 'relay'` on both peers hides each player's IP from the opponent ([LiveSwitch](https://developer.liveswitch.io/liveswitch-server/server/security.html)). The costs are 100% TURN usage (still $0 on Cloudflare) and a hard dependency on the relay. Under GDPR, IP addresses are online identifiers and therefore personal data (Recital 30; CJEU *Breyer*) ([activeMind](https://activemind.legal/legislation/gdpr/recital-30); [Bird & Bird](https://twobirds.com/en/insights/2016/global/cjeu-decision-on-dynamic-ip-addresses-touches-fundamental-dp-law-questions)). The owner therefore needs a privacy notice, a lawful basis such as legitimate interest, and the provider's DPA, and should log as little as possible. No server fixes cheating. Pure P2P stays host-authoritative, and only a server-authoritative WebSocket relay changes that.

## Conclusion

The cheapest viable path costs **$0/month**. The owner creates a Cloudflare account and a TURN key, and deploys one free Worker that mints short-lived TURN credentials (rate-limited, ideally only after a direct attempt fails). The same Worker's Durable Object can host one-shot signaling rooms with 128-bit IDs, which replaces copy-paste. The current copy-paste flow stays as the fallback. The robust path costs **$5/month**: the same stack on Workers Paid, which lifts the daily caps that otherwise make operations fail at midnight-UTC boundaries. It adds spend notifications and a privacy notice, and optionally a hibernating Durable Object relay as a last-resort fallback or as the future home of server-authoritative rules. Self-hosted coturn and the 500 MB TURN tiers make sense only with a specific reason, such as avoiding Cloudflare, because they add patching or a $39-99 step up and do not improve on a $0 anycast relay.

The more surprising point is that the decision is architectural, not financial. At this scale, $5 or less buys any of the three designs. What separates them is who holds the trust and the data. TURN keeps the peers in control and the content encrypted. A signaling server adds a party that could tamper with the handshake. A WebSocket relay hands the server everything, but in return it can stop a cheating host. Before committing, the owner should spend an afternoon measuring one real match's bytes and re-checking the flagged prices. Neither result is likely to change the recommendation, but both turn the estimates into facts.
